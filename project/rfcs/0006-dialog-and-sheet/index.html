RFC 0006: Dialog and Sheet

  • Status: accepted
  • Kind: component
  • Proposed: 2026-11-24
  • Decided: 2026-11-24

Summary

Section titled “Summary”

Adds the modal surfaces: a Dialog centered over the screen, an alert dialog that asks people to confirm something consequential, and a Sheet that rises from the bottom edge or slides in from the end edge. They share one anatomy, one focus model, and one set of tokens, including a new scrim and the overlay shadow. It is for any app that interrupts a task to ask for something or to confirm it.

Problem

Section titled “Problem”

Modal surfaces are where accessibility most often breaks: focus escapes behind the dialog, is not returned when it closes, or lands on a destructive button; a screen reader reads the page behind; nothing says how to get out. Every app also has to decide how a modal looks on a phone and on a large screen, and the three platforms answer differently. iOS presents a modal task as a sheet; Android and the web usually center a dialog.

An example: deleting a saved address asks “Delete this address?” with Cancel and Delete. The Delete button must not be where focus starts, Escape must mean Cancel, and on iOS people expect the system’s own alert.

Proposal

Section titled “Proposal”

Kinds and presentations

Section titled “Kinds and presentations”
Kind Presentation For Dismissed by
Dialog Centered; sizes sm, md (default), lg A short task: rename, choose, fill in a few fields Escape, the scrim, the close button or Cancel, the back gesture
Alert dialog Centered; sm Confirming something consequential or irreversible Escape, Cancel, and the back gesture, all as Cancel. Not the scrim
Sheet edge: bottom (default) or end A longer task, or one that keeps the screen behind in view As a dialog, and by dragging it away on touch screens
  • On a narrow window a dialog spans the width less a margin, and an end sheet comes from the bottom instead.
  • On iOS every dialog and sheet is presented as the system sheet, which is how iOS presents a modal task: from the bottom on iPhone and centered on iPad. An alert dialog on iOS is the system alert. See Platform notes.

Anatomy

Section titled “Anatomy”
Part Required Notes
Scrim Yes Covers the screen behind and makes it inert
Container Yes Raised surface with a hairline outline
Title Yes heading-md. It names the dialog. It wraps; it is never truncated
Description No A sentence under the title that the dialog is described by
Body No Any content. It scrolls between the header and the footer when it does not fit
Footer No (yes for an alert dialog) Buttons, at the end edge, the primary action last. They stack, primary first, when they do not fit in a row
Close button When the footer has no Cancel An icon-only ghost Button at the end of the header. Its name is given by the app
Drag handle Bottom sheets on touch platforms A visual cue only. Dragging is never the only way to close

Every dialog and sheet has a visible way out: a Cancel in the footer or the close button.

Focus

Section titled “Focus”
  • On open, focus moves into the dialog: to the title by default, so a screen reader starts at the top and nothing is activated by accident. The app can name another target, such as the first field of a form. In an alert dialog focus starts on the least destructive action, usually Cancel.
  • While open, focus stays inside, and everything behind is hidden from assistive technology.
  • On close, focus returns to the control that opened it. If that control is gone, the app says where focus goes.

Scrolling

Section titled “Scrolling”

The header and footer stay in place and the body scrolls. A hairline appears under the header and above the footer while content is scrolled beneath them. When the window is too short to show the header, the footer, and three lines of the body, as at 400% zoom, the whole container scrolls as one instead.

The scrim and the shadow are overridable; the panel widths are not, like every other size. shadow.none is the primitive RFC 0005 adds.

New primitives, which need no RFC: color.scrim.40, .60, and .80 (neutral.1000 at 40%, 60%, and 80% opacity), and dimension.400, .560, and .800. The token pipeline already accepts colors with alpha; the scrim is never a background for a contrast check, because what is under it is unknown.

Motion: the scrim and a dialog fade in; a sheet slides in from its edge. With reduced motion, everything fades and nothing moves.

Usage rules that will go into the guidelines:

  • Use a dialog for a task that must be finished or abandoned before going on. If people need the screen behind to do the task, use a sheet or put it in the page.
  • Use an alert dialog only to confirm something consequential. Its title says what will happen (“Delete this address?”), and its confirming button repeats the verb (“Delete address”), as the Button guidelines ask of danger buttons.
  • Do not open a dialog from a dialog. Replace the content, or close the first.
  • Do not use a dialog for a message that needs no decision. A toast (RFC 0008) or an inline message interrupts less.

Where the systems disagree, and which way Raincoat goes:

  • First focus. Pajamas starts on the title, Cloudscape on the close button, Fluent on the first control, and Carbon on the first input or the primary button. Raincoat starts on the title, as Pajamas does: a screen reader begins at the top, and a keyboard user is one Tab from the first control. Starting on a primary button risks confirming by accident. In an alert dialog, Raincoat starts on Cancel, as Carbon does for destructive modals.
  • Escape in an alert dialog. Fluent allows only the footer buttons; Spectrum treats Escape as Cancel. Raincoat follows Spectrum: Escape is the keyboard’s way out everywhere, and Cancel is always safe.
  • The scrim. Pajamas always closes on an outside click, Carbon only for passive modals, Spectrum only when there is a close button, and Fluent for modal dialogs but not alerts. Raincoat closes standard dialogs and sheets on the scrim, and never an alert dialog, where a stray tap must not count as an answer.
  • Close button and Cancel together. Pajamas and Carbon always show the close button; Spectrum says a dismissible dialog has no footer buttons; Fluent adds Close only when there is no Cancel. Raincoat follows Fluent: one visible way out, not two that mean the same.
  • Scrolling. Carbon and Fluent keep the header and footer fixed; Pajamas avoids scrolling inside the modal. Raincoat keeps them fixed, and lets go of them at very large zoom, as Fluent does for drawers.
  • Role. Cloudscape uses alert for its modal. The others keep alertdialog for alert dialogs, as Raincoat does.

Internationalization

Section titled “Internationalization”
  • Right-to-left. Footer buttons, the close button, and the end sheet all sit at the end edge, which is the left. Nothing is placed by left or right.
  • Long and expanded text. Titles and descriptions wrap. Buttons stack when their translated labels do not fit in a row; snapshots in the expanded pseudo-locale show the stacked footer. Cloudscape notes that translations can be three times as long as English.
  • Vietnamese and CJK snapshots show a dialog with a two-line title and an alert dialog in each locale.

Platform notes

Section titled “Platform notes”
  • Web. shadcn/ui’s dialog, alert-dialog, and sheet sources, adapted, on Radix, which the package already depends on. Radix traps focus, hides the page behind from assistive technology, and locks scrolling. There is no swipe to dismiss on the web; Radix does not offer it, and adding a library for it is not worth a gesture that must never be the only way out.
  • iOS. Dialogs and sheets use SwiftUI’s sheet, sized to their content with presentation detents, with the tokens inside it. The system draws the drag indicator, closes the sheet on a swipe or VoiceOver’s escape gesture, and lets the app refuse while there are unsaved changes. An alert dialog is SwiftUI’s alert, with the confirming button given the destructive role where it destroys something. The system alert follows the person’s text size, bold text, and contrast settings, and is what people on iOS recognize as a request to confirm. The cost is that the theme does not reach it: on iOS an alert looks like the system’s, not the brand’s. This follows ADR 0016: where the platform provides a behaviour people can configure, use it.
  • Android. Compose’s Dialog window with content built from the tokens. A sheet is a dialog window whose content is dragged with Compose Foundation’s anchored dragging, because Material’s bottom sheet would add the Material dependency ADR 0016 leaves out. The system back gesture, including predictive back, closes a dialog or sheet as Cancel.

Migration

Section titled “Migration”

None. These are new components.

Alternatives considered

Section titled “Alternatives considered”
  • Themed alerts on iOS. One look on every platform, but a custom alert does not follow the system’s accessibility settings as fully as the system’s own, and people on iOS know the system alert.
  • A side sheet as its own component. It shares every part and rule with the bottom sheet, so it is an edge, not a component.
  • Full-screen dialogs on phones. Material offers them for long tasks. A bottom sheet covers the same need and can still be dismissed by dragging.
  • Swipe to dismiss on the web. shadcn/ui’s drawer does this with an extra library, vaul. It adds a dependency for a gesture that is never required.
  • Starting focus on the first control. Radix’s default. It reads well for a form, which is why the app can choose it, but in a dialog that starts with a link or a destructive button it puts focus somewhere surprising.
